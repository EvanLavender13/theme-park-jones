# Research: measured-runtime

## How should repeated launches be summarized, and a change told from noise?

Benchmark noise comes in levels: between repetitions inside one process, between launches of the same binary, and between builds. Kalibera and Jones show that repetition is needed where the variance is, and for native code on a desktop the launch level usually dominates: memory layout, frequency scaling, and other processes differ per launch, so many repetitions inside one launch make a confident but wrong number. The tools agree on the shape that follows. Each launch is summarized on its own, and launches are the samples a comparison treats as independent. Medians are preferred to means because a stray slow repetition, from a context switch or a page fault, moves a mean and not a median. hyperfine reports median, min and max beside the mean, and flags outliers by their distance from the median in median absolute deviations rather than standard deviations, for the same reason. A per-tick distribution needs its tail kept apart: the worst tick is what a player feels as a hitch, and a median hides it by design.

Three published ways decide whether two sets of launches differ. Google Benchmark's compare.py runs a Mann-Whitney U test, rank-based and free of any assumption about the distribution, and warns below nine repetitions. Criterion.rs bootstraps the difference, tests it at a significance level, and also ignores changes inside a noise threshold of about 1%. Kalibera and Jones give a confidence interval for the ratio of execution times, reported as "faster by 5.5% ± 2.5%". All three accept a false alarm rate per comparison, typically 5%. A report that compares several stages on several parks makes many comparisons at once, so at 5% each an unchanged tree would routinely show some change as significant.

The simplest rule that suits a report rather than a gate is non-overlap: summarize each launch by its median, and call a stage's change clear only when every launch of one report lies beyond every launch of the other. With ten launches on each side and nothing changed, launches are exchangeable, and the chance that all ten of one fall on one side of all ten of the other is 2 in 184,756, so even dozens of stages compared at once almost never show a change that did not happen. The price is sensitivity: a change smaller than the launches' spread is never called clear, which matches Marr's finding that changes under about 5% are rarely distinguishable from noise on an ordinary machine anyway. A drift that makes one whole report slower, such as thermal throttling or a busy machine, defeats any rule, which is why paired runs in alternating order, before and after taking turns, are the stronger design when both trees can be built side by side.

Rejected: a significance test per stage, such as Mann-Whitney or a bootstrap at 5%. Across many stages and parks it marks changes clear in an unchanged tree, against the capability's criterion that it never does. Rejected: a fixed percentage threshold alone, such as Criterion's noise threshold. It ignores the spread actually measured, which differs between stages and between debug and release. Rejected: means and standard deviations. One slow launch inflates both, and timings are skewed, never normal. Rejected: pooling every repetition of every launch into one sample. It treats repetitions as independent when the launch they share moves them together, and understates the spread.

## How should an in-process stage timer be built so its numbers do not lie?

The clock is std::chrono::steady_clock, the standard's monotonic clock. libstdc++ maps high_resolution_clock to system_clock, which can jump with the wall clock and has shown about a millisecond's resolution on MinGW, while its steady_clock on MinGW-w64 is backed by QueryPerformanceCounter, with sub-microsecond resolution. A tick of this park takes from microseconds to tens of milliseconds, so each tick and each stage call can be timed on its own rather than in batches.

Repetitions inside a launch are a fixed count, not a time budget. Criterion and Google Benchmark grow the count until a time target is met, which suits microbenchmarks of nanosecond calls but makes two reports time different amounts of work. Here every stage is long enough to time singly, and a fixed count means two reports measure the same calls. The first call of a stage pays for cold caches and first allocations, so warm-up repetitions are run and discarded before the counted ones, as hyperfine's --warmup and Criterion's warm-up phase do. Stepping is the exception: a tick changes the world, so ticks are timed in sequence from the loaded park, and the park itself must already be warm, with guests spread out and shops working, or the first ticks measure a park that plays unlike a full one.

An optimizing compiler can delete work whose result is never used. Google Benchmark's DoNotOptimize and ClobberMemory stop it with compiler-specific inline assembly. A runner that times whole stages can avoid that machinery by using each result visibly: a mesh's vertex count, a candidate's hash, the world's hash after stepping, written to the report. Output that depends on the result keeps the work, needs no compiler-specific code, and doubles as a check that both builds timed the same work.

Windows adds machine noise the tool cannot remove. Power plans change benchmark results by about a fifth, and turbo frequency varies with temperature and load, raising run-to-run variance severalfold. Timer resolution settings such as timeBeginPeriod affect only sleeps and waits, which a runner that never sleeps does not use. So the report states what it ran (build, park, launches, repetitions), and the practice is to run on mains power, an idle machine, and one power plan, and to read only changes that clear the spread measured under those conditions.

Rejected: high_resolution_clock. On libstdc++ it is the wall clock. Rejected: time-targeted repetition counts. They change the work timed between reports, and every stage here is long enough to time singly. Rejected: compiler barriers in inline assembly. Using each result in the output does the same job portably. Rejected: raising the process's priority or pinning it to a core from the runner. It needs platform-specific code in the one module the rest of the project keeps free of it, and alternating launches with a measured spread already absorb the interference it would reduce.
