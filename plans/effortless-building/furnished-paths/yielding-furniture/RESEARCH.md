# Research: Yielding Furniture

## How to lay pieces along a curved verge that yields to obstacles

The milestone's RESEARCH.md settles the pattern: spread pieces evenly over each free stretch, at a spacing as close to the set one as fits, as Cities: Skylines 2's Line Tool does over a whole line. A search for procedural street-furniture placement found tools that place objects by rules aligned to a road's direction and curvature, such as Houdini road generators, but no published method for the even spread with obstacles. Street guides ask only that furniture stay out of the walking width and the operating space around it. The approach below comes from the park's own geometry.

The paved surface already knows each segment's outline. Its body runs straight, and at a point that exactly two segments use, the outline turns from one segment's side to the next segment's side through a fillet, a taper's cubic curve, or a step. If each side's line follows its body and then half of the turn at each such end, furniture runs on around bends and tapers. Two segments' furniture meets in the middle, and each piece keeps its own segment. At a junction the outline between two arms is split the same way, so pieces follow the junction's edges and none stands across an arm's opening. At a free end, the line stops where the body stops.

Finding where a piece can stand is one test: does the piece's footprint, grown by a small margin, overlap any obstacle polygon? Paving pieces, walkway pieces, box and entrance footprints, door frontage strips, and earlier pieces are all convex polygons of at most four corners, and the park's edge is a square. Testing a piece at places along the line every half meter finds every place it can't stand. The places sit at whole multiples of half a meter, measured from the segment's From point along its body, so a change at one end of a side doesn't move where the rest of it is tested. An obstacle blocks a run of places at least a piece's width long, which is longer than the half-meter step. Halving between each free and blocked place then finds the stretch's ends to under a millimeter. The test treats the segment's own paving as an obstacle too. On the inside of a tight bend, that keeps pieces off the paving without a special case.

A uniform grid over the park holds the obstacles, so each test reads only the obstacles near it. The work then grows with the length of the furnished sides and with what lies near them, never with segments times boxes.

Rejected: building the verge from the surface's edge lines. Edge lines belong to points, not segments. They are cut where other paving hides them, so a side can't be read back from them. Rejected: projecting each obstacle exactly onto a straight verge. Projection is exact on straight parts, but a piece on a curve straddles many short pieces of the outline, so curves would still need the sampled test. Rejected: stopping every side at the segment's ends. Inserting a point into a furnished path would then leave a gap of several meters in its furniture.

Sources: https://merttasci.artstation.com/projects/3EV4mE — a procedural road system that places street furniture by rules aligned to road direction and curvature; https://www.hamilton.ca/sites/default/files/2022-09/pedpolicies-street-furniture-guidelines.pdf — keeping furniture out of the walking and operating space.
