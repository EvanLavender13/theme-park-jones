# Research: Wide Overlay

## Where should an overlay show a value that belongs to path places?

City and park builders that show a value per stretch of their network color the network itself. Cities: Skylines' traffic view colors roads from green to red by how well traffic flows, and its service views color the roads green where they are covered and gray where they are not. Planet Coaster's heatmaps shade areas of the park, and its players asked for the heatmap to be projected onto the paths, so a path would glow where guests on it share a thought. Both point the same way: the player reads the value where the guests stand, on the path.

This game's food availability is a value at path places, and the overlay so far shows it on the ground beside each guest path, under the paving. That worked while every path was 3 m wide and the band reached 4.5 m past the paving. Once a segment can be 10 m wide or more, the paving covers most of the band, and the shading is hidden exactly where the paths matter most. Shading the paving itself removes the problem, because the paving is already drawn at each segment's width, through tapers, rounded corners, and joins.

The direct way to shade the paving is to draw the guest paving's own pieces in the ramp's colors. The pieces are exactly the paving the player sees, so every spot of it is shaded at every width, taper, corner, and join, with no reach to size. A segment's piece can run the segment's whole length, so it is cut across its line at the places the overlay samples, about every meter, and each cut takes the value at that place on its segment's line, interpolated between cuts as the band's rows are today. A junction's pieces belong to its point and take, at each corner, the value at the nearest place on the point's segments. Walkway pieces and backstage pieces are left out.

The band's rule was that each spot shows the value at its nearest guest path place. On the paving, the plainer rule is that each spot shows its own path's value: the value at the place on that segment's line nearest the spot. The two differ near a junction, where a wide street's paving runs on past a narrow side path's line, and a spot there can lie nearer the side path's line. Shading it with the side path's value would put the side path's color on the street. A junction's own paving belongs to its point, so it takes the nearest of the point's segments. Where paving of unjoined paths overlaps, as where they cross, each piece's shading lies lower the farther it is from its own line, all at one rate per meter, as today's band does, so the depth test shows the nearest line's value. The fall must fit between the paving and its edge lines, and stay steep enough for the depth test to tell nearby lines apart. The hover tooltip must use the same rule, so it explains the value under the pointer.

The overlay's zero is gray today. Guest paving is tan, but backstage paving is a blue gray close to the zero color, so a guest path with no food reachable would look like a backstage path. Zero needs a color distinct from the ramp, from guest paving, and from backstage paving.

Rejected: a band beside each path sized by the network's width at each place, the slice's first plan for this member. The shading stays under the paving and next to shops, where paths and boxes cover it, and the value still reads as belonging to the ground beside the path. Rejected: today's tent band, cut to the guest pieces on the CPU or masked to them with a stencil on the GPU. It reaches a fixed 6 m from its line, but a segment can be 16 m wide and a sharp corner's outer paving lies farther still, so its reach would have to be sized to the paving. Shading the pieces themselves covers the paving by construction. Rejected: one flat color for each surface piece. A segment's piece runs its whole length, so a 60 m street would show one value and lose the gradient along it.
