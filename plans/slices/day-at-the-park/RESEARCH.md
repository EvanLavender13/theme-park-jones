# Research: day-at-the-park

## How do park and life-sim games model guest needs, and how are needs answered?

RollerCoaster Tycoon 2, read from OpenRCT2's source, keeps hunger, thirst, toilet, energy, nausea, and happiness as private counters on a 0 to 255 scale, each moving toward a target value every few seconds. The needs are coupled inside the guest. Digesting lowers hunger, raises the toilet need, and props up energy, so a fed guest's energy holds and only falls once its stomach is empty. Eating raises the toilet need and lowers thirst. Drinking only lowers thirst. Sitting on a bench restores energy and relieves nausea, but also raises thirst and the toilet need. Walking costs energy. Guests act on a need only past a fixed threshold, such as a toilet need of 160, picking one pressing need at random and walking to the nearest matching facility by straight distance, ignoring queue and price. Benches are never sought: a guest sits only if the tile it steps onto has one and it is tired, nauseous, or carrying food. Time on a bench and in a toilet scales with how great the need is. A toilet empties the need one unit per tick. Penalties for unmet needs are small happiness losses, and OpenRCT2 shipped without them for years without players noticing, so in RCT needs mostly drive revenue rather than mood. A park-wide warning appears when a sixteenth of guests want a facility they are not heading to.

Parkitect has tiredness from walking, answered by benches tired guests look for, and a toilet need raised by eating and drinking that wears happiness down while unmet. Toilets are small shops that get dirty and are refused when full of dirt. Planet Coaster 2 has eight needs, among them energy, which falls faster with long walks and queues and is restored by benches, and a toilet need that builds slowly and faster after drinks. Planet Coaster 1 lets shop ingredients change needs, such as salt raising thirst. The Sims has bladder drain faster while eating or drinking, with an accident when it reaches zero, and every toilet is a single user.

The Sims' objects publish advertisements of which motives they satisfy and by how much, and a Sim scores every advertised action through piecewise-linear response curves per motive, with appeal falling with distance. That is direct precedent for guests that read only the offers facilities publish (principles 3 and 6) and for decision 0019's scored choice. Game AI Pro notes that taking the top score always looks robotic and a weighted pick sometimes looks foolish, which the softmax temperature already balances.

Players' complaints are about needs they cannot see the cause of, and about needs that reduce to a checklist. Parkitect guests walk past rows of benches while tired, and hungry ones ignore stocked stalls, because the cause, a commitment elsewhere or empty stock, is invisible. RCT's benches feel pointless because guests never seek them. Planet Coaster guests refuse toilets with cash in hand, blamed on hidden per-category budgets. The guides' advice comes down to benches at intervals and toilets by every ride exit, so uniform coverage dominates and needs become upkeep. Needs become decisions when they couple to each other and to space: food fills the bladder, long walks tire guests, facilities placed where guests come out catch them, and guests differ. Making each guest's causes visible, and aggregating them into park-wide statements such as how many guests cannot find a toilet, is what players credit.

For this slice this suggests needs coupled inside the guest and stated in its inspector (drinks and meals fill the bladder, walking tires), offers that guests weigh at a distance so benches and toilets are chosen rather than stumbled on, and an aggregate of why guests left as the park-wide statement.

Rejected: threshold-triggered seeking with one need picked at random — a gate, and the nearest facility by distance alone ignores the offer. Benches noticed only underfoot — they publish no offer, so guests cannot choose them. Hidden couplings the guest cannot state — untraceable (principle 8). Hidden budgets that refuse offers — untraceable. Dirty toilets refused outright — a gate; dirt lowering an offer's appeal fits principle 5 and belongs to a later slice with staff. Accidents as an event at zero — a cliff; out of scope, and if added later a risk that rises smoothly with a recorded cause. Strict urgency buckets, as The Sims groups motives — a gate on which options are considered. Guests changing each other's state, as RCT's vomit contagion does — bypasses the medium. Ingredient levers that make misery profitable, as Theme Park's salt does — rewards cynicism.

## How do guests decide to leave?

No shipped park game found makes going home a scored option among the guest's others. RCT keeps every guest whose energy, happiness, and cash are above fixed floors, and otherwise rolls about a 5% chance per check to leave, so stays end when the stomach empties and energy falls, or happiness or cash run out. Its stays have no time limit. Planet Coaster 2 accumulates a hidden park fatigue whose rate depends on the guest's mood bucket, with desperate guests, those with a need very low, leaving fastest; it is the closest to a gradient. Parkitect guests stay longer when happier and leave when broke or exhausted. Two Point Hospital and The Sims' visitors leave at a threshold. Disney Research's ParkSim draws each visitor's stay length from an empirical distribution and walks it to the exit when it ends, which is what this project's fixed stay does today.

In discrete-choice terms, going home is the outside option, and scoring it against offers with terms for tiredness, unmet needs, and closing time would be new for the genre. It keeps leaving a gradient (principle 5) and states its cause in the choice record (principle 8), and the largest of its terms at the moment a guest picks it names why the guest left. RCT shows that a coupling of food to energy can make stay length follow how well the park feeds its guests without any explicit rule.

Rejected: floors followed by a random roll, as RCT does — gates, and the roll hides the timing's cause. A fatigue rate switched by mood buckets, as Planet Coaster 2 does — quantized steps where a continuous term fits. A stay drawn from a distribution — the fixed stay this slice replaces; it ignores the park. Instant exit when any need reaches zero — a cliff.

## How do park games handle the day, opening hours, and closing?

Classic RCT has no day and night; a calendar day passes in about ten seconds. RCT3 has opening hours set only in the scenario editor, and at closing it jumps the clock to the next opening with guests left where they stand. Planet Coaster 1 lets the player set hours, fast-forwards the sky through closed hours, and by players' reports guests do not leave at closing, so the hours are cosmetic. Planet Coaster 2 has a 24-hour clock with an optional park day in which guests arrive in the morning and leave at closing, at about 12.5 real minutes per game hour, slow enough that players rarely see night. Its patches fixed bugs from the closing exodus: guests trapped behind full exit queues and needing transport rides to get out. Parkitect's developers judged day and night low priority for gameplay and offer only a cosmetic night toggle. Cities: Skylines 1 decouples its visual day from the calendar at 4.5 to 9.5 real minutes, and RollerCoaster Tycoon World's cycle of a few real minutes drew complaints, with players asking for 30 to 60. Cities: Skylines 2 runs an hourly simulation and lets the visual cycle be switched off while the simulated time of day keeps running.

So hours are either cosmetic or drive arrivals and departures, and making them drive both is the version with gameplay. Closed time is either jumped, which snaps the sun and leaves guests standing overnight, or run faster. A deliberate day length tied to the clock reads as coherent, and running more ticks per frame through the night keeps the simulation identical while the renderer follows the clock. Night too dark to build in is the most common complaint across these games, and most add an opt-out.

Rejected: jumping the clock over closed hours — guests stay put overnight and the light snaps. Cosmetic hours — no decision for the player. A visual day decoupled from the simulated clock — incoherent. A day of a few real minutes — complained about as too fast.

## When do real park guests arrive and leave?

TouringPlans' traffic patterns for Disney parks show early arrivals at opening, a lull, then about two hours of heavy arrivals peaking in the late morning, a diminishing stream until early afternoon, and the most guests in the park in the early afternoon. Departures overtake arrivals from mid-afternoon in waves, with a large exodus at closing. A park's peak in-park count is typically 40 to 65% of its day's attendance, and up to 80% at Tokyo Disney, where stays run eight to nine hours of a fourteen-hour day. A regional waterpark's stay is about three and a half hours. ParkSim models arrivals as an empirical rate for each hour of the day, scaled by the day's attendance. No published share of arrivals in the first hour was found.

This suggests an authored arrival curve over the opening hours that rises after opening, peaks in the late morning, tapers by early afternoon, and stops before closing, with stays of several game hours so that many guests leave before close and closing still sends the rest home. In the simulation it is a piecewise-linear table (decision 0020), and a deterministic way to turn a rate into guests is to accumulate it and admit a guest each time the total crosses a whole number, or to draw arrivals from the keyed draws.

Rejected: a steady rate while open — no rush or quiet spell to plan for.

## How do stylized games render day and night cheaply and readably?

The sun's direction follows the time of day as an hour angle, with its elevation rising and falling between sunrise and sunset, tilted so noon is never straight overhead and flat-shaded faces stay distinct. Its intensity fades toward the horizon. At night no light comes from a sun below the ground; a dim, cool moon light from above the horizon replaces it. A hemispheric ambient, mixing a sky color and a ground color by a surface's facing, keeps faces distinguishable when the sun is gone, which is what keeps flat-shaded boxes readable at night. Inigo Quilez's outdoor rig adds a warm bounce light opposite the sun.

Stylized games author these as color ramps keyed on time of day: sun color and intensity, sky and ground ambient, sky zenith and horizon, and fog, each a keyframed table or a row of a small gradient texture. Firewatch builds its sky from a three-color gradient with sun and horizon halos so artists work in color rather than physics. Alto's Adventure blends its ambient from white by day through orange at sunset to blue at night, with an unlit sky. Animal Crossing shifts its sky palette by the hour.

Film's day-for-night practice keeps nights readable: lower brightness, desaturate, and tint shadows and midtones cool while keeping highlights neutral, without washing the frame blue or letting shadows go black. In the engine that is an ambient floor at night that is cooler and less saturated, not only darker. Uniform dimming produces the pitch-black nights players complain about in Cities: Skylines and Planet Coaster, and a saturated blue wash makes every palette look alike.

The renderer reads the time of day from the simulation's clock and never the reverse, and any trigonometry for the sun stays in the renderer, which may use the C runtime (decision 0022). If the simulation needs night, it computes it from its own clock.

Rejected: dimming everything uniformly — unreadable, pitch-black nights. A saturated blue wash — flattens the palette. Lighting from a sun below the horizon — looks wrong. Wall-clock time anywhere near the simulation — breaks determinism (principle 10). Physically based sky models — more than flat-shaded boxes need.
