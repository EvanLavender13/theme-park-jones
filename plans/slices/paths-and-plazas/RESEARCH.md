# Research: paths-and-plazas

## What do Cities: Skylines 2's Platter and its neighbors do, and what carries over?

Platter is not a surface or area tool. It is a zoning overhaul for Cities: Skylines 2. The player places parcels, rectangles of zone cells, anywhere and at any angle, with a chosen width and depth. A parcel snaps to the road edge and to other parcels, can sit back from the road by a setback, and the building that grows on it faces the parcel's own orientation. It is popular because vanilla zoning builds one rectangular grid per road segment, which leaves wedges and gaps along curves. Giving each building its own frame, attached to the road edge with a setback, makes curved streets work. That is the prior art for boxes snapping along paths: each box takes its frame from where it meets the path, rather than from a grid the path owns.

Its neighbors show the rest of the toolset players reach for. Cities: Skylines 2's own road tool has straight, curve, continuous, and grid modes, and snaps to existing geometry, round lengths, right angles, building sides, and guidelines extended from other roads, each toggleable; its wiki warns that guideline snapping left on breaks layouts. Its surfaces and districts are drawn polygons. AreaBucket fills an area bounded by roads in one click. Line Tool places objects along lines, curves, and circles with editable control points after placement. Move It exposes a segment's ends and control points for reshaping. Anarchy, which turns off placement checks wholesale, is one of the most used mods, which says players resent validation that blocks them.

So snaps should be offered visibly, each one suppressible, and never forced. Boxes should take their frame from the path edge they meet, with a setback. Validation should block only what is physically impossible (principle 5).

Rejected: one grid per path for boxes along it — leaves gaps on curves, the problem Platter exists to fix. Guideline snaps on by default — break layouts.

## Why is Planet Coaster 2's path system disliked?

Planet Coaster 1 built paths in fixed segments up to 8 m wide, and players made plazas by tiling grid squares. Planet Coaster 2 replaced this with a line builder with a width setting and a merge toggle, a stamp builder for geometric shapes that scale and snap together, a draw tool for plaza outlines, rounding and edit tools, and the old segments as a classic mode.

Players' complaints have common causes. Paths fuse with nearby paths unexpectedly and distort finished work, because merging is by proximity and a new path rewrites old ones. Paths snap with snapping turned off, because some snaps are not covered by the toggle. Joins leave seams, holes, and see-through triangles, because merges are mesh unions rather than derived topology. Deleting removes more than intended, and the paint brush cannot touch merged areas, because once merged a stroke loses its identity. Height snaps to about 0.25 m so queues drift, and paths on uneven ground do not mesh. Queues cannot branch, since the line tool only extends. Three build modes write different kinds of state, and mixing them in one preview left paths at different heights and trapped hundreds of guests. On wide paths and plazas guests walk single file or zig-zag, stall in the middle, and loop.

Planet Coaster 1's crowd system explains the last: walkable space is a voxel grid in 4 m chunks, and each goal has a flow field guests follow. Any paved area is walkable, but distance is a property of the grid, and a plaza gives guests no structure beyond the field.

The lesson for this slice: the saved intent must stay the strokes and outlines the player made, with merges, joins, and seams derived from them every time, so nothing is ever rewritten behind the player's back and every stroke stays editable on its own. Every snap must be visible and suppressible. Every tool mode must save the same kind of intent.

Rejected: merging by proximity that rewrites existing intent — edits what the player did not touch. Merged areas saved as one mesh — strokes lose their identity. Hidden or unconditional snaps — break symmetry and trust. Several modes saving different state — mismatched results. Extend-only drawing with no branch from a path's middle — queues and side paths become impossible.

## How do other games make plazas, wide paths, and buildings along paths?

Plazas built from path pieces, as in Planet Coaster 1, Planet Zoo, and Parkitect, show holes, seams, junction clutter, and distances that depend on the tiling. Plazas drawn as areas, as in Planet Coaster 2's draw and stamp tools and Cities: Skylines 2's surfaces, are cleaner to author, and fail in Planet Coaster 2 only where the area merges into the path mesh and guests have no structure on it. Cities: Skylines 1's pedestrian plazas are a painted policy over road-width networks, not open walkable ground.

Width works as a property of a path: Jurassic World Evolution 3 has width tiers that raise capacity, Planet Coaster 2 a width scale, Two Point Campus a slider, and Cities: Skylines 2 path widths. Parkitect shows the opposite: wider paths are more tiles, so guests walk more tiles and tire faster, and guests circle on wide paths; players fake width with decorative covers over one real path.

Buildings along curves work best with a frame per building anchored to the path: Platter's parcels with setbacks, and Anno 117's buildings that rotate to the road beside them and connect by a corner. They fail with one grid per road, as in vanilla Cities: Skylines 2, and with plot corners the player tunes by hand, as Manor Lords' burgage plots show: players spend long minutes fixing corners that jump across the road.

Tiny Glade and Townscaper are the art-director model in its purest form: tiny intent, and hand-written rules that resolve it into arches, stairs, and fitted modules.

Rejected: plazas tiled from path pieces — bricklaying, seams, and tiling-dependent distance. Wide paths as parallel paths — inflate walked distance and seam. Plot corners tuned by hand — the bricklaying this game avoids. Grids that quantize freeform angles, as Anno 117's subtiles do — at odds with gridless building.

## How can a plaza be part of a route graph while distance stays along the routes travelled?

The options are navmeshes, flow fields, medial axes, and visibility edges. A navmesh, as Recast and Detour build, rasterizes geometry into convex polygons, and the funnel algorithm finds the true shortest path through a corridor of them; its polygons come from voxelizing geometry, not from intent. Flow fields, as in Continuum Crowds and Planet Coaster 1, suit many agents heading to a few goals on open ground and handle avoidance too, but distance becomes the grid's metric. A medial axis, as Valhalla routes plazas and the Explicit Corridor Map structures crowds, gives natural walk-down-the-middle routes but overstates distance against the straight line. Routing only around a plaza's perimeter, as OpenTripPlanner does without area visibility, overstates it more.

The fit for principle 4 is portals and visibility edges. Each place a path or a door meets the plaza's edge is a portal. The network gets a derived edge between each pair of portals whose length is the shortest walk between them inside the walkable area: the straight line when nothing is in the way, and otherwise the shortest line around the reflex corners of the outline and the obstacles inside it, a visibility graph over those corners. With k portals that is k squared edges, cheap for plazas of a few portals; OpenTripPlanner caps area visibility at 200 nodes, which warns that large detailed areas need bounds. The path graph stays the authority for distance, and plaza edges rebuild whenever the outline or its connections change. Local motion inside a plaza, such as spreading guests across it, can be layered on later without defining distance.

Rejected: a navmesh or flow field as the authority for distance — distance becomes an artifact of the rasterization. A medial axis — routes longer than guests would walk. Perimeter-only routing — overstates distance and hides why guests go round.

## How do Cities: Skylines' road tools model nodes and segments, and what does that suggest for paths built from nodes?

Both Cities: Skylines games save networks as nodes and segments. A segment is a Bezier curve between two nodes, with two control points shaping it, and it carries a road type that lays its lanes. A node is an end, a junction, or a middle node joining two segments of the same type. Cities: Skylines 1 also inserts a middle node every 96 m on long roads. Nodes count against hard limits of 32,768 nodes and 36,864 segments, so players prune unnecessary ones with mods. Pathfinding runs on the lanes laid along segments and joined at nodes. Middle nodes still matter to behavior there: cars change lanes, and pedestrians step from a footpath to a sidewalk, only at nodes. Shaping detail and routing are coupled, which is why pruning nodes changes traffic.

The popular mods are all node and segment manipulation. Move It exposes a segment's two end nodes and two curve control points for dragging, with snapping to a straight line. Network Multitool adds, removes, merges, and splits nodes, puts a node where two segments cross, arranges nodes on a circle, evens the slope between nodes, builds parallel roads, fits a curve between two segments, and reverses a segment. Node Controller changes how a node joins its segments: as a middle, bend, stretch, crossing, or end node, with each segment end's offset, shift, rotation, slope, and stretch adjustable. That is mostly the node's visual footprint. Intersection Marking Tool draws markings and fillers, paved or grass areas, between points on a node's segment ends, so a filled area belongs to a node and follows it. Intersection assets are prefabricated arrangements of nodes and segments. Once placed they become ordinary roads, and each piece can be removed or changed on its own.

Planet Coaster 2 is the contrast. Its line, stamp, and draw tools all add to one merged path surface. Overlaps join into a single shape, and stamp delete cuts shapes out of it. The shape under the hood is that merged result, and it shows when something is deleted. Cities: Skylines never has this, because every piece of road surface belongs to exactly one segment or one node. Two roads that overlap are resolved by a node where they cross, never by merging their surfaces.

For this project, that suggests intent made of nodes and segments, with every tool writing them. A drawn stroke becomes a chain of nodes and segments. A stamp is a prefabricated arrangement of them, like an intersection asset, and is ordinary once placed. Delete acts on a node and the segments attached to it, or on one segment. Paving is derived per segment and per node footprint, so no surface is ever owned by a merge, and deletion removes exactly what the player sees as that piece. A wide paved area could be a node with a large footprint that segments join at its edge, shaped the way Node Controller and Intersection Marking Tool shape junctions. That is one option among several.

Two layers follow from Cities: Skylines' lesson. The control layer holds every node the player edits, including bend and middle nodes that exist only for shape, texture, or material changes. The simulation graph contracts the control layer: chains of degree-two control nodes collapse into one edge whose length is the summed curve length. Only ends, junctions, and attachments such as doors stay nodes. Adding a node to shape a path then changes nothing about routing or the graph's size. navigable-networks' carriers already work this way, with nodes only at endpoints, junctions, crossings, and connections. With node-based intent, a carrier would span several segments, and a place on it would need a stable key from the segments it covers.

Rejected: a saved merged surface, as in Planet Coaster 2 — overlaps become hidden shapes that show on delete. Every control node as a simulation node, as Cities: Skylines 1's middle nodes are — shaping detail inflates the graph and changes behavior. Resolving overlaps by merging surfaces — a crossing node keeps each piece separately owned.
