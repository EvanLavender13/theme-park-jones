# Research: good-paths

## Why is Planet Coaster 2's path system disliked, and what does that require of this model?

Planet Coaster 2 replaced fixed path segments with a line tool, stamps, a draw tool, and edit tools. All of them add to one merged path surface. The complaints follow from that:

- New paths fuse with nearby paths by proximity and distort finished work.
- Some snaps still apply with snapping turned off.
- Joins leave seams, holes, and see-through triangles, because a merge is a mesh union, not a join the game knows about.
- Delete removes more than intended, because a merged stroke has lost its identity.
- The line tool only extends from an end, so queues and side paths can't branch.
- The build modes save different kinds of state, and mixing them trapped guests.
- On wide paths, guests walk single file, zig-zag, or stall.

Updates added square corners, terrain following, and invisible paths. None changed merging by proximity, delete overreach, or single-file walking.

For this model, the findings suggest the following. Join only where the player ends a path on a point or segment, or where a new path crosses one of its own kind. Never join by nearness alone. Show every join in the ghost before commit. Save points and segments as intent and derive the paved surface from them on every change. Then delete removes exactly one segment, and no seam can exist that the surface rules didn't make. Let a path start from any point or any place on a segment, so branching is ordinary. Make the one suppress key cover every snap without exception. Spread guests across the width so wide paths look used.

Rejected: merging by proximity — it rewrites work the player didn't touch. Saving the merged surface — strokes lose identity and delete overreaches. A suppress key that leaves some snaps on — players stop trusting snaps. Extending only from ends — queues and side paths can't branch.

## How do the best tools make visible snapping feel good?

SketchUp's inference engine is the model most players of building games have met. It snaps the cursor to points (endpoints, midpoints, intersections) and to directions (axes, parallel, perpendicular). Each kind has its own color and a short screen tip, such as "Midpoint". Pausing over a point tells SketchUp to prefer alignments from it. Shift locks the current direction, so the cursor can move elsewhere and still stay on that line. While drawing, the player can type an exact length and press Enter. Bier and Stone's snap-dragging is the origin of this style. It builds temporary alignment lines from points the user marked as of interest and snaps to them and to their crossings.

AutoCAD's object snap tracking uses the same idea. Hovering an object snap point acquires it and marks it with a small plus. Alignment paths then run from acquired points at 0, 90, 180, and 270 degrees. Hovering the plus again removes it. When several snaps apply at one place, Tab cycles through them. The search radius is an aperture in screen pixels.

Figma draws red guide lines and spacing labels when an object aligns with another. Holding Ctrl (Windows) turns off all snapping for the drag. tldraw, an open-source canvas, shows the details. Its snap threshold is 8 screen pixels, divided by the zoom to get world units. It snaps the x and y axes separately and takes the nearest snap on each. After moving to the snapped place, it collects every alignment that now holds and draws them all, not only the one that won.

Cities: Skylines 2's road tool snaps to existing geometry, 8 m cell lengths, 90 degree angles, building sides, guidelines from other roads, and the zone grid. Each snap has its own toggle. The community advice for clean grids is to keep geometry, length, and 90 degree snaps and turn off guidelines, which break grids. Its tooltips show length, angle, and slope rounded to whole numbers. A popular mod shows decimals, because a rounded readout hides when a length is off by a fraction. Parkitect and Planet Coaster 1 build paths on tiles or fixed pieces, so they have little to teach about free snapping.

Platter, a zoning mod for Cities: Skylines 2, is the prior art for frontage. Each parcel snaps to the road edge at a setback and takes its frame from the road it meets. Anno 117 turns buildings to face the road beside them.

These findings answer the detailed questions:

- Priority. When several snaps apply, the tools pick the one needing the smallest correction, with points before lines. A point that satisfies two snaps, such as a crossing of two guides, beats either alone. A sensible order for this model is existing points, then crossings of guides, then existing segments, then angle and straight-on guides, then whole-meter length along the chosen direction. Length snapping fits best as a refinement after direction is fixed, not as a competitor.
- Stickiness. Plain snapping warps the cursor to a target, and places near the target become unreachable. Baudisch and others measured this in snap-and-go. Hysteresis is the common fix: keep a snap until the cursor leaves a release radius somewhat larger than the capture radius. If the release band is too wide, small deliberate nudges stop working, so keep it modest.
- Radius. Measure capture in screen pixels and convert to meters by the zoom. A fixed radius in meters glues everything when zoomed out and nothing when zoomed in. ArcMap, AutoCAD, and tldraw all default to pixels.
- Guides. Draw each active snap as its own visible guide: a dashed line for a direction, a marker on a snapped point or segment, and an arc or tick for an angle. Draw every guide the final position satisfies. Give each kind a distinct color so the player can tell them apart.
- Readouts. Show the segment length while drawing, and the angle when an angle snap is active. Show the exact value, so a snapped whole-meter length reads as exact and an unsnapped one doesn't look snapped.
- Suppression. One held key that turns off every snap for the drag is the Figma convention, and it fits this model. Release the key and snapping resumes at once.
- Frontage. Derive a shop's facing and setback from the segment's center line and width, not from the drawn outline. Then the result is exact and doesn't depend on rendering.

Rejected: a snap radius in meters — it fails at both ends of the zoom range. Guideline snaps from distant paths — Cities: Skylines 2 players turn them off because they break layouts. Rounded readouts — they hide lengths that are slightly off. Snaps without a visible guide — the player can't tell why the cursor moved. Separate toggles for each snap as the only control — they cost a trip to a menu where one held key is enough.

## How do node-and-segment path or road editors behave?

Cities: Skylines 1 and 2 save roads as nodes and segments. A segment runs between two nodes and is shaped by Bezier control points. A node is an end, a junction, or a middle node joining two segments.

- Crossings. Building a road across another at grade makes an intersection there. The game inserts a node and splits the crossed segment in two. The ghost shows the new intersection before commit.
- Continuing and branching. Dragging from an existing end continues the road. Starting on a segment's middle splits it and starts a branch.
- Deleting a segment. The bulldozer removes one segment. If a node is left with no segments, it goes too. If a node is left with two, it stays as a plain middle node. In Cities: Skylines 2, segments trimmed to fit an intersection grow back to full length when it goes. If the regrown shape would collide, the game refuses the delete. Players find that baffling.
- Deleting a node. Neither game can remove a middle node and merge its two segments. Network Multitool's remove-node mode does that, and its union mode merges two nodes into one. Cities: Skylines 2 players ask for the same and use mods.
- Corners. There is no per-node radius. Node Controller adjusts each segment end's offset, rotation, and segment at a node, mostly to shape the visible footprint. Its corner offset sets how far each road end sits back from the node. Curves come from Bezier handles, which Move It exposes for dragging.
- Undo. Neither game has undo in vanilla. Players asked for it from release. Mods add it: Undo It for Cities: Skylines 1, City Rewind for Cities: Skylines 2. Undo It rebuilds roads, but not what was attached to them, such as bus stops. Move It's Cities: Skylines 2 port can't undo deletes.

Planet Coaster 2 has a line tool that extends from ends, a merge toggle, a delete brush, and an edit tool that drags the border of a drawn area. It has no shared points to drag and no segments to delete one at a time. Two Point Campus paints paths on tiles, with a perimeter mode and a line mode for diagonals. Anno 117 paints roads on tiles with an optional diagonal mode, and players report unwanted extra corners. Parkitect paths are tiles. None of these three offer points, segments, or per-corner shaping. Figma gives each vertex its own corner radius, with zero meaning sharp.

These findings suggest the following for this model:

- Auto-split at a crossing, as Cities: Skylines does, and show each new junction in the ghost.
- When a segment is deleted, remove points left with no segments. Keep a point left with two segments as a corner, with its radius. Never refuse a delete because neighbors would have to change shape, since their shape is derived.
- Deleting a point between two segments has two plausible meanings: merge them into one straight segment, or remove both. Network Multitool chose merge. Merging needs a rule for the width when the two widths differ. Removing both is simpler but deletes more than the player pointed at. The choice is the user's.
- A per-corner radius is simpler than Bezier handles and keeps every segment straight. The arc uses up a tangent length of R times tan(θ/2) on each segment, where θ is the turn angle. If the two tangent lengths don't fit within the segments, the radius has to shrink to fit, as drawing tools clamp corner radii. The inner edge has radius R minus half the width. If R is smaller than half the width, the inner edge folds back on itself, so R needs a floor or the inner corner needs another rule. If the two segments differ in width, the edge has to taper across the arc.
- Undo of a build that split a segment must restore the original segment. Its key will be fresh. The safest undo record holds the intent before and after the edit, not a list of steps to reverse. If other things refer to a segment by key, such as a shop's door connection, a fresh key breaks the reference. Undo It shows that failure. Either the undo record re-points such references, or they resolve from geometry each time.

Rejected: Bezier handles on every segment — more control than straight segments with corner radii, and they need explaining. Refusing a delete when neighbors would reshape — Cities: Skylines 2 players find it baffling, and derived geometry never needs it. No undo — the most requested missing feature in Cities: Skylines 2. Tile-painted paths as in Parkitect, Two Point Campus, and Anno — this game has no grid.

## How to build and render the union of widened segments with arcs at corners, filleted inside corners at junctions, square ends, and an outline, in C++ with light dependencies?

Clipper2 is the standard polygon library. It is C++17, under the Boost Software License, and about 300 KB of source in four .cpp files and eight headers, with CMake support. Its clipping core works on int64 coordinates and is exact. Its offset code computes normals and arcs with sqrt, sin, cos, acos, and atan2 in doubles, then rounds to integers. ClipperOffset offsets open paths with butt ends, which give square ends, and joins bends with round or square joins. Union then merges every segment into one surface with holes, and its boundary is the outline. Inside corners can be filleted by morphological closing: offset out by r, then in by r. Closing has two flaws here. It uses one radius for the whole set, so a radius that grows with width needs per-junction work. It also bridges any gap narrower than 2r and fills small holes, so two separate paths that run close together fuse. That is the Planet Coaster 2 failure. The library's new triangulation module is described by its own author as buggy.

Road games build junctions per node instead. A/B Street's approach is documented: thicken each road's center line, trim each road back where its thickened shape collides with its neighbors, and form the junction polygon from the trimmed ends, sorted around the node. Cities: Skylines does the same with a node mesh that fills the space between trimmed segment ends. For this model the per-node method is direct:

- Sort a point's segments by angle.
- For each pair of neighbors, intersect the right edge of one with the left edge of the next.
- Fillet that corner with a circle tangent to both edges, with a radius that grows with the widths.
- Trim each segment back to the farther of its two tangent points.
- The junction is a polygon of trim lines and fillet arcs, and it is star-shaped around the point.

A point with exactly two segments uses its corner arc instead. A point with one segment gets a square end. Each piece triangulates directly: segment bodies are quads, arcs are strips, and junctions are fans from the point. No general triangulator is needed for the fill. A/B Street reports trouble when roads are nearly parallel or very short, because trims grow large or overlap. This model needs a rule for both cases, such as clamping the fillet when trims would cross.

The outline doesn't need a union either. Map renderers draw road casings first and fills second: every piece widened by the outline width in the edge color, then every piece at true width in the fill color. Where pieces meet, fills cover the casings, so the edge line runs only along the outer outline of the joined surface. This needs one pass of each per path kind, and nothing else may draw between the two passes. In a depth-tested 3D scene, casing and fill share a height, so they need draw order, a depth bias, or a stencil instead of depth to settle which shows. If two same-kind segments overlap without a shared point, the casing method still draws a clean union, but no fillet forms between them. That clean union hides the overlap, which the slice wants shown. path-network's RESEARCH.md replaces casings with edge strips along each piece's outline edges, drawn over the fills.

Signed distance fields can draw the same surface in a shader. The distance to an oriented box gives a square-ended segment, a ring sector gives a corner arc, and a circular smooth minimum fillets where shapes meet. The outline is the band where the distance is near zero, and antialiasing comes free. The costs are real. Each fragment must check every segment near it, which needs tile binning on the CPU. Smooth minimum blends any two shapes closer than its radius, so it bridges separate paths, as closing does. Baking the field to a texture limits outline sharpness to the texel size, and a park-sized texture at centimeter detail is too large.

Speed and locality. A ghost rebuild on every pointer move needs geometry in well under a frame. With the per-node method, a segment's trim at a point depends only on that point and its segments. Moving a point changes its own junction, its segments, and the junctions at their far ends, and nothing else. A crossing split adds one point and changes the split segment's two ends. A ghost can rebuild only those pieces and draw them over the committed mesh. A Clipper2 union over hundreds of segments is a sweep over a few thousand vertices, likely around a millisecond, but it is global unless split by tiles or by connected group. This estimate needs measuring. Determinism matters only if the geometry feeds the simulation. If the surface is drawn only, and routes and frontage come from center lines and widths, float differences between builds can't change the simulation.

If a general triangulator is ever needed, earcut.hpp is header-only, ISC-licensed, fast, and handles holes. It favors speed over triangle quality.

Rejected: Clipper2 union with closing for fillets — it bridges nearby separate paths and uses one radius everywhere. Clipper2's triangulation — its author calls it buggy. A signed distance field as the main method — it needs per-fragment binning, bridges nearby paths, and is harder to read and test than plain geometry. Baking the field to a park-sized texture — too large for sharp outlines. Deriving the sim's frontage or routes from the drawn surface — it ties the simulation to render math.

## How do games spread pedestrians across a path's width without crowd simulation?

Planet Coaster 1 moves guests by flow fields over 4 m chunks. Lanes form when crowds meet, and stuck guests shrink their collision radius until they pass. That is crowd simulation, which this slice leaves out. Parkitect's guests circled on wide paths. Its developers added a fix that worked on two- and three-tile paths but less well on plazas. Cities: Skylines puts pedestrians on fixed sidewalk lanes at set offsets from the center line.

The cheap method fits this model. Give each guest a fixed sideways offset, taken from a hash of its key. Store it as a fraction of the half-width, not in meters, so a guest stays on the paving when it moves to a narrower segment. On a corner arc, the offset follows the parallel curve. Across a width change or a junction, blend the offset over a short distance so guests don't jump. A small slow drift along the walk, also from the hash, keeps rows from looking ruled. If the offset only affects drawing and picking, it needs no simulation state. If guest positions ever feed the simulation, the offset must stay deterministic.

Rejected: flow fields per goal — they are crowd simulation and still leave wide areas without structure. Random offsets drawn each frame — guests jitter. Offsets in meters — guests leave narrow paths.
