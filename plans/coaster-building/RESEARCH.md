# Research: coaster-building

## Why do piece and spline builders produce abrupt force changes?

A rider feels the track's curvature times speed squared, plus gravity. If curvature jumps at a joint, force jumps at that instant. Joints come in grades: tangent-continuous (G1) looks smooth but lets curvature jump; curvature-continuous (G2) makes force continuous but lets its rate of change jump; G3 makes that rate continuous too. Pendrill's loop papers state the coaster case directly: a straight running into a circular arc gives an instant onset of the full force, and smooth-looking track can still have discontinuous second derivatives.

Roll has the same problem one level up. A builder that ramps bank linearly within each piece has a constant roll rate per piece, so roll acceleration jumps at every joint. Railways learned this with the clothoid, which fixes curvature but still has abrupt roll changes at its ends, and moved to higher-order transition curves. Roll also has to happen about the heartline, at the rider's chest. Rolling about the rail swings the rider's head sideways, which they feel as lateral force, and that lateral grows with roll acceleration, so roll rate has to be continuous and not just roll angle.

Speed is the third cause. The bank that cancels lateral force depends on speed squared over radius, so the same geometry rides differently at different speeds. A piece that is smooth at one entry speed spikes at another, and a builder that works in geometry needs re-smoothing whenever speed changes upstream.

Planet Coaster's pieces are defined by end states (length, pitch, yaw, bank) joined at G1, which is why its track needs a smoothing pass. Players report that smoothing tightens curves, can undo custom inversions, adds odd banking on straights, and wrecks a hand-built heartline. The PC2 force mod, ProTrack, which matches the force mod Evan has used, holds one vertical and one lateral force target over a whole piece and ramps bank linearly per metre. That explains Evan's helix trouble. A descending helix speeds up continuously, so holding a constant radius needs force and bank that keep rising, and a static force per piece cannot express that.

NoLimits 2's vertex editor has the same issue in another form. Its Bézier vertices each affect the neighbouring segments, unequal handle lengths leave jerk after smoothing, and its pre-made inserted loops are criticised for "pumping": the radius swings back and forth through the element.

Rejected: Geometric pieces joined by end state, plus a smoothing pass — joints are only G1, so force and roll rate jump, and smoothing damages what it touches. Static force per piece with linear bank — wrong whenever speed changes within a piece, and still steps at joints. Smoothing roll after the path is built — moves the rider off the heartline the path was built for, creating laterals unless the path is re-solved.

## How does force vector design work, and what tools use it?

Force vector design builds track by integration. The user describes how the ride should feel over a stretch, and the tool steps a point forward along the heartline, bending its path by those forces at the speed it simulates (including friction), then offsets the rails from the heartline. Because the force description is continuous, the track is smooth by construction. The Twente thesis cites an industry source calling it the main method of real layout design, and the NoLimits wiki says manufacturers use it.

FVD++, the reference tool, has four section types. A straight section has optional banking. A curved section is an arc with radius, angle, and lead-in and lead-out. A force section keyframes normal force, lateral force, and roll rate. A geometric section keyframes pitch rate, yaw rate, and roll rate. Each section runs against time or distance, chosen per section, and its graphs are built from transition shapes (linear up to quintic, sinusoidal, plateau) with controls that warp them. Each new section inherits the previous one's end curvature, so sections join smoothly. Roll is a rate, so constant bank means a flat roll graph.

The FVD++ manual documents two traps. First, roll measured against the world is undefined at vertical pitch, and makes a slowing constant-force helix spiral inward. Roll measured relative to the track behaves, but then the helix's world bank drifts as speed changes. Second, time-based pitch rates stretch the track as speed rises on a drop, and create bumps at low speed. The manual recommends distance-based sections where speed varies.

Its strength is smoothness: users report a polished loop in minutes. Its weakness is position. Forward integration does not land anywhere in particular, so reaching an endpoint or avoiding an obstacle takes many iterations and expert knowledge, and errors grow along long sections. KexEdit added a gradient-descent optimizer because hand-tuned roll rarely returns to exactly zero. FVD also computes the forces at the centre car only, and builders report end seats running much harsher; one reported 0 g hills reaching about -1.1 g in the end seats. Community advice is to underestimate forces for that reason.

The tools in this lineage:
- Newton 2, the original FVD add-on for NoLimits 1, which FVD++ credits.
- FVD++ (openFVD on GitHub, GPL-3.0), the standalone editor described above. Its licence would bind this project to the GPL if its code were reused.
- KexEdit, an open-source, MIT-licensed FVD editor. It is described in detail below.
- ProTrack, the PC2 mod, which credits KexEdit and openFVD. Its code holds one vertical and one lateral force over a whole PC2 piece, with roll per metre fixed by the piece's bank change, stepped at 100 Hz. It has no licence file.
- Elementary for NoLimits 1, which offered formula elements (parabolic hills with a target airtime force, half-loop and half-corkscrew formulas). Builders joined halves into dive loops and Immelmanns by matching radii, and mirroring an element left a flat spot.

KexEdit is the closest existing match to the track engine this capability would need. Track is a node graph. An anchor sets position, orientation, speed, heartline offset, friction, and air resistance. Force sections keyframe roll rate, normal force, and lateral force. Geometric sections keyframe roll rate, pitch rate, and yaw rate, with pitch about a horizontal axis and yaw about world up, and a steering option that applies roll after pitch and yaw so rolling does not change the layout. Curved sections take a radius, an arc, an axis, and lead-in and lead-out. Copy-path re-runs an existing path's shape from a new anchor, and reverse nodes run track backwards. Each section is keyed by time or distance, but integration always steps at a fixed 100 Hz. Values carry over from one section to the next, so a geometric section after a force section starts with the curvature the force section ended on. Keyframes interpolate as constant, linear, or weighted Bézier, with easing presets from sine to quintic.

Every roll in KexEdit is a heartline roll: each point stores the heartline position, and the rail sits 1.1 m below it by default. Speed comes from energy conservation, minus friction proportional to distance travelled (a constant coefficient that ignores load) and air resistance proportional to speed cubed. An optimizer runs gradient descent on one keyframe until roll, pitch, or yaw at a chosen point hits a target. The bridge node, which its docs call Catmull-Rom, is in code a cubic Bézier along the two ends' directions with blended roll: purely geometric, with its forces unchecked, and its docs advise keeping the two ends close. There is no element library: the repository has easing presets, two sample tracks, and copy-path, and nothing else for reuse.

On its main branch the track math lives inside Unity's entity systems, so reusing it would mean lifting the integrator out. A newer branch, fresh-start, rebuilds the core as a Unity-free Rust library whose simulation layer is pure math with no dependencies, evaluated as a pure function of the graph, and tested against analytical cases (projectile motion, energy conservation, a closing loop, a banked turn in equilibrium). That branch also prototypes trajectory optimisation aimed at hitting targets. This project's track math has to be deterministic C++ across builds, so KexEdit is a reference for the integration and its tests, not a dependency; the MIT licence permits reusing its code with attribution either way.

Rejected: Pure forward force integration with no fitting — cannot hit positions, and roll rarely returns to zero. A bridge spline as the general way to connect track — even KexEdit's own docs limit it to short, simple gaps. Designing forces for a centre-car point mass only — front and rear seats ride much harsher. Time-based geometry across speed changes — stretches track and bumps at low speed. Roll measured against the world — undefined at vertical pitch.

## How are the elements shaped, and what force profiles do they have?

The evidence is uneven. Loops and airtime hills are well studied; most inversions have no published force profile at all, and what exists is single community posts.

The vertical loop is the best understood. A circular loop that is weightless at the top pulls about 6 g at the bottom, with an instant onset, and a longer train makes it worse. Stengel's clothoid loop (Revolution, 1976) joins different radii with spiral pieces so the force builds gradually, giving the familiar teardrop. Physicists have also worked out loops of constant normal force analytically. Stengel's own view is that perfectly constant force is boring: changing forces are part of the thrill. A measured inverted loop peaked around 4 to 4.5 g at the bottom and about 2.2 g at the top.

An airtime hill shaped like a parabola matches free fall and gives roughly 0 g, a floater. A crest tighter than the parabola gives negative force, an ejector. Sources disagree on the thresholds; one database calls -0.25 to +0.25 g floater, -0.25 to -0.75 g "flojector", and below -0.75 g ejector. Builders say parabolas are good for floater but poor for sustained ejector.

A helix is naturally described by geometry, not force. Its slope and radius define it, and because speed changes as it climbs or descends, its bank must keep changing to cancel laterals. FVD++ builds one from a geometric section: a pitch rate for constant slope, a yaw rate for the circle, and small roll-rate changes for the bank.

The inversions are compositions of simpler parts:
- A corkscrew is a 360° roll that a rider feels much like a helix. No published force profile was found; builders place roll points at entry, middle, and exit and fix spikes by hand.
- A zero-g roll is a 360° twist over a near-weightless crest. One community method builds a 0 g hill, banks it about the heartline so the heartline stays where the unbanked track ran, and adds lead-in and lead-out to avoid lateral spikes. The force at its apex depends on speed and is disputed.
- A heartline roll keeps the rider's path nearly straight while the train rotates around them.
- A dive loop and an Immelmann are the same element run in opposite directions: a half loop combined with a half twist. One community recipe holds lateral force at zero throughout, takes normal force from about 3.6 g down through 0 g and back up to 3 g, and rolls 30° briefly and then 150° over the rest. Its author called it not very good.
- A cobra roll is two half loops facing the same way, joined by two half corkscrews twisting in opposite directions. No force recipe was found.
- For inline twists, one builder advises keeping roll rate under about 70 to 80°/s, after 113°/s produced about 2.5 g laterals.

Turns and RMC elements:
- A wave turn is a 90° banked turn with a small hill inside it.
- An overbanked turn banks past 90°, often 100 to 120°. An outerbank banks away from the turn, turning lateral force into negative vertical force; sources on how it feels are weak.
- A zero-g stall twists 180° on the climb and stays inverted briefly at the crest; RMC introduced it in 2014.
- A step-up under-flip is an inverting hill that rolls 270° against the turn.
- Community builders describe RMC as pulling at least 1 g laterally for split seconds but never holding it, and cite 1.25 g from press material. No numbers were found for what makes a transition "whippy".

Rejected: Circular arcs joined straight to straights — instant onset and about 6 g at a loop's bottom. Fixed pre-made elements dropped in anywhere — they pump, and are tuned for one speed (the speed point is inferred, not sourced). Mirroring or stitching half-elements without matching curvature — leaves flat spots and radius jumps. Perfectly constant force everywhere — Stengel calls it boring, and friction makes it impractical.

## What force limits do real rides respect?

The standards limit force as a function of how long it lasts, not as single caps. ISO/TS 17929, as quoted by Pendrill and Eager, limits onset to 15 g/s, requires force to fall off at 0.8 g/s or faster after a sustained peak, and allows 6 g along the spine for at most 1 s and 5 g for at most 2 s, with combined directions bounded by egg-shaped envelopes. ASTM F2291 also uses magnitude-against-duration curves, filters data at 5 Hz, ignores events shorter than 200 ms, and treats sustained laterals of 0.5 g or more as needing restraint consideration. EN 13814 caps force at 6 g, measured 60 cm above the seat.

Practical figures are rougher. Pendrill puts children's rides near 2 g, family rides near 3 g, and big rides above 4 g, mostly below 5 g. The Twente thesis designed against 5 g, -1 g, ±1.5 g lateral, and 15 g/s. Sources disagree sharply on comfortable laterals: one forum claims B&M and Intamin stay at or below 0.4 g, while RMC cites 1.25 g and wooden coasters are put at 1 to 1.8 g. Secondary numbers for F2291 (about +6 g, -2 g, ±1.5 g lateral) come from a blog and are unverified.

What this suggests for a game: intensity is a matter of magnitude and duration together, and onset rate matters on its own. That fits rating a ride by its force history rather than by peak values alone.

## Can track and custom inversions be composed from reusable authored parts?

Every existing approach composes something, but none composes parametric elements that join cleanly. RollerCoaster Tycoon and OpenRCT2 compose fixed, authored pieces on a grid (half loops, quarter loops, half corkscrews, zero-g rolls, dive loops), and new shapes arrive as new assets, with no dials. NoLimits 2 inserts pre-made elements, which pump. Elementary composed formula halves, and builders had to match radii by hand and lived with flat spots where halves were mirrored. FVD++ and KexEdit compose authored transition shapes inside sections, with continuity inherited from section to section; that is the reliable part, but the player still writes every force curve.

Academic work has tried generating whole layouts. Campbell and Verbrugge generated RollerCoaster Tycoon tracks with Markov chains and reinforcement learning, and found reinforcement learning gave more control over excitement, intensity, and nausea. Schuttert's Twente thesis optimised parametric Bézier elements (drop, hill, banked turn, loop, pitched turn) with reinforcement learning, and hit the problems that matter here: the agent assumed any twist was possible and produced a mechanically infeasible tight turn, getting feasible output often took several attempts, and every parameter change it tried made results worse. No SIGGRAPH-style work on coaster element generation turned up; that is an absence in these searches, not proof that none exists.

The structure of real inversions supports composition. Corkscrews, zero-g rolls, dive loops, Immelmanns, and cobra rolls all decompose into half loops, heartline rolls of some angle, and turns. What the prior art lacks is a rule that makes the joins clean. The research suggests one, untested: if every authored part ends in a canonical state (roll rate zero, a known normal force, roll at 0° or 180°), and parts are integrated in the track-relative frame about the heartline over distance rather than time, then any sequence of parts joins with continuous curvature and roll rate by construction. A custom inversion would then be a sequence of parts, each already riding well. Fitting a part's dials to its entry speed, so that it exits level or at the intended heading, is a small one-element fit of the kind KexEdit's optimizer does. That is a different and much smaller problem than routing track between two points in space, which this research found no tool doing well.

Rejected: Fixed pieces with no dials, as in RollerCoaster Tycoon — every new shape is a new asset, and nothing adapts to speed. Stitching formula halves by hand — joins need manual radius matching and leave flat spots. Unconstrained generative or learned layouts — produce spatially infeasible elements and need repeated attempts. Automatic routing between arbitrary track ends — no tool does it well; KexEdit's bridge is limited to short, simple gaps.

## Where can measured force traces of real elements come from?

Manufacturers measure every ride with instrumented test runs for certification, but publish none of it. Public measurements come from two places.

The Ride Forces Database collects enthusiast recordings made with the Ride Forces app on iOS or its browser recorder: about 7,000 recordings of about 1,000 rides, 749 of them coasters, across about 200 parks. Each recording shows vertical, lateral, and longitudinal force over time with playback, and can be downloaded as CSV or the app's own formats. Recordings are whole rides, not split by element, so finding a corkscrew means locating it in the trace by its timing. The site smooths the accelerometer data and warns that exact values should not be trusted, because calibration varies and a phone can shift in a pocket; patterns repeated across several recordings of one ride are what it says to rely on. Seat position, sample rate, and sensors beyond the accelerometer are not stated, and no licence or terms of reuse were found.

Physics-education researchers publish a smaller number of research-grade recordings. Pendrill's group measured launched coasters with loops, Kanonen and Speed Monster, using three-axis accelerometers riding with the train.

What this suggests: the database is a good reference for tuning recipes by eye. For example, comparing a recipe's force profile against several recordings of a real cobra roll shows whether its shape and timing are plausible. With no stated licence, reading the traces is safe; copying data into the game is not, without asking the site's owners.

Rejected: Treating a single recording as ground truth — the site itself says single recordings can be flawed.

Sources: https://rideforcesdb.com/ — recording counts, traces, downloads; https://rideforcesdb.com/about — recording method, smoothing, accuracy caveats; https://physics.gu.se/LISEBERG/eng/pe8_5_003.pdf — research-grade three-axis measurements on launched coasters.
